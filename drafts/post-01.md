Most organisations are running their AI pilot in the finance team.

It makes sense. Finance has laptops, corporate email, one working language, and a manager
who can absorb a bad output without anyone getting hurt. It is the easiest possible place
to succeed.

It is also about ten percent of the working world.

The other ninety percent are on a shop floor, in a warehouse, on a ward — or, in my case,
behind a wheel. No corporate email. Several first languages. A shift pattern that does
not accommodate a two-hour training webinar. And consequences that are physical rather
than reputational.

I lead people and fleet operations at Ecosine Transports in Dubai. Our workforce is
[[HEADCOUNT]] professional drivers running 140 electric vehicles. Over the
last [[TIMEFRAME]] we have put two AI assistants into live operation with them. Not a
pilot. Production, with real consequences when it goes wrong.

Three things that environment taught me, none of which appear in a vendor deck:

→ The model was never the hard part. Rewriting [[TIMEFRAME]] of accumulated operational
knowledge into something a system could actually reason over took [[PROPORTION, e.g.
"four times"]] longer than the build.

→ Adoption is a trust problem, not a training problem. Our usage went up after we made
the assistant less capable — because we gave it permission to say "I don't know" and
route to a human.

→ A confidently wrong answer to someone operating a vehicle is not a bug report. We
designed the escalation path before we designed a single answer.

None of this is theoretical for me. It is what my week consists of.

So starting today I am going to publish it — every other day, for the next sixty days.
What worked, what it cost, and the parts that failed twice before they worked once.
Frontline AI, EV fleet economics in the Gulf, and building people systems that
operations teams will actually use.

If you are deploying AI to a workforce that does not sit at a desk, I would genuinely
like to know what broke first for you.

Because I promise it wasn't the model.

## Why this post is built this way

| Element | Choice | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening line** | A flat, specific observation about other people's behaviour | Makes a claim the reader can immediately test against their own organisation. No "excited to share." |
| **Lines 1–4** | Concede the opposing view before dismantling it | "It makes sense" buys enormous credibility in an executive register. You are not attacking finance teams; you are widening the frame. |
| **"about ten percent"** | Deliberately approximate | You are not citing a study. Approximation stated as approximation is more honest — and more defensible — than a precise-sounding figure you cannot source. |
| **Credentials paragraph** | Placed fourth, not first | Earning the right to speak before stating the qualification. Leading with a title reads as a bio; leading with an argument reads as a point of view. |
| **"Not a pilot. Production."** | Two-word sentences | The single most differentiating claim in the post. It gets its own beat. |
| **Three arrows** | Scannable, each one a future post | This post seeds P02, P15 and P06. Readers who engage here are pre-qualified for those. |
| **"published every other day"** | Explicit commitment | Converts profile visitors into followers. Only include it if you will hold the cadence — breaking it publicly costs more than never promising. |
| **Closing two lines** | Question, then a short punch | "Because I promise it wasn't the model" gives commenters something to agree or disagree with. Open questions get no replies; contestable statements get many. |
