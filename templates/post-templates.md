# Post Templates

Four reusable frameworks, tuned to a polished executive register. Each has an anatomy,
a fill-in skeleton, and a worked example.

## Rules that apply to all four

1. **The first 210 characters are the entire post.** LinkedIn truncates behind "…see
   more" on mobile. If the hook doesn't earn the tap, nothing else matters.
2. **One idea per post.** Two good ideas in one post is one wasted post.
3. **Single-line paragraphs.** White space is the format. Dense blocks are not read.
4. **Never open with a greeting.** No "Excited to share", no "Thrilled to announce",
   no "In today's fast-paced world". These are the four most reliable scroll signals
   on the platform.
5. **No external links in the body.** LinkedIn suppresses reach on posts with outbound
   links. Put the link in the first comment and say so.
6. **Close with a question you actually want answered.** A CTA the author doesn't care
   about produces comments the author doesn't answer, and the algorithm can tell.
7. **Numbers over adjectives.** "Significantly improved" is invisible. "From 11 days to
   4" is the post.

## Template 1 — Short-form Insight
*200–400 characters of body. Highest frequency, lowest effort, strong for comment volume.*

### Anatomy

```
[HOOK — a specific claim, stated flatly. One line.]

[CONTEXT — the situation that produced it. Two to three lines.]

[NUMBER — the evidence. One line.]

[PRINCIPLE — the generalisable lesson. One or two lines.]

[QUESTION — directed at the reader's own operation.]
```

### Why this shape works

The flat opening claim does the stopping. The context earns the right to the claim. The
number makes it defensible. The principle is what gets screenshotted and shared. The
question converts a reader into a commenter — and comment velocity in the first hour is
the strongest single input to LinkedIn's distribution decision.

### Skeleton

```
We [[DECISION YOU MADE]].

[[WHAT WAS TRUE BEFORE — the situation everyone recognises]].
[[THE MOMENT IT STOPPED WORKING]].

[[METRIC]] moved from [[BEFORE]] to [[AFTER]].

[[THE PRINCIPLE — stated as a general truth, not as a boast]].

[[QUESTION ABOUT THE READER'S OWN OPERATION]]
```

### Worked example

> We stopped measuring training by completion rate.
>
> Everyone was completing. Almost nobody was competent.
> Completion measures whether someone clicked through. It has never measured whether
> they can do the job.
>
> We switched to supervised competency checks at day 30. Our "trained" number dropped by
> [[FIGURE]]. Our incident rate dropped with it.
>
> A metric that only ever goes up is not a metric. It is a comfort.
>
> What does your organisation actually measure — completion, or capability?

## Template 2 — Long-form Narrative
*1,300–1,900 characters. Two to three per month. This is what builds authority.*

### Anatomy

```
[HOOK — a contrarian or counterintuitive claim. One line, hard stop.]

[STAKES — why the reader should care. One or two lines.]

[SETUP — the situation, with real operational specifics.]

[TURN — the moment your assumption broke. This is the load-bearing paragraph.]

[EVIDENCE — three to five data points, arrow-bulleted.]

[COST — what it took, what you got wrong, what you'd do differently.]

[PRINCIPLE — the transferable lesson, stated cleanly.]

[QUESTION — an invitation to compare notes, not a request for validation.]
```

### Why this shape works

Executive-register long-form fails in one specific way: it stays abstract. The TURN and
COST sections are the antidote. Disclosing what you got wrong is what separates an
operator writing from a consultant writing — and it is the cheapest credibility available
to you, because nobody fabricates their own failures.

### Skeleton

```
[[CLAIM THAT CONTRADICTS THE CONVENTIONAL VIEW]].

[[WHY THIS MATTERS TO THE READER'S P&L OR RISK]].

[[TIMEFRAME]] ago we [[WHAT YOU SET OUT TO DO]]. [[THE OBVIOUS APPROACH EVERYONE TAKES]].

Then [[THE THING THAT DIDN'T WORK]].

What the data showed:
→ [[DATA POINT 1]]
→ [[DATA POINT 2]]
→ [[DATA POINT 3]]

[[WHAT YOU CHANGED]]. [[WHAT IT COST — time, money, or credibility]].

[[WHAT YOU'D DO DIFFERENTLY IF YOU STARTED AGAIN]].

[[THE PRINCIPLE]].

[[QUESTION INVITING PEERS TO COMPARE]]
```

### Worked example — opening 6 lines

> Range anxiety is a management problem, not a battery problem.
>
> Which matters, because most fleets are solving it with capital when they could solve it
> with a roster.
>
> When we electrified, we did what everyone does: we specified vehicles against our
> longest route, added margin, and built a business case around range.
>
> Eighteen months in, range has almost never been our constraint. Scheduling has.

## Template 3 — Carousel / Infographic
*8–14 slides. Highest save rate on the platform. One to two per month.*

### Anatomy

| Slide | Content | Design rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title + the promise. Nothing else. | Largest type in the deck. Readable at thumbnail size. |
| 2 | The problem, in one sentence. | Sets up why slides 3+ matter. |
| 3–N | **One idea per slide.** A heading, one number, one line of support. | If a slide needs a paragraph, it is two slides. |
| N+1 | The synthesis — what it all adds up to. | The screenshot slide. Make it the best-designed one. |
| N+2 | CTA + your name and handle. | Every carousel gets reshared without context. Sign it. |

### Non-negotiable specifications

- **1080×1350 (4:5 portrait).** Square wastes 25% of available mobile screen height.
- **Export as PDF**, upload as a document post. Image carousels are deprecated.
- **Minimum 24pt equivalent type.** The most common carousel failure is unreadability at
  phone size, not weak content.
- **One accent colour, one neutral, one background.** Three colours total.
- **Number your slides** (3/10). Progress indication measurably increases completion.

### Skeleton

```
Slide 1:  [[NUMBER]] [[THINGS]] [[THAT PRODUCE THE OUTCOME]]
Slide 2:  Most [[AUDIENCE]] [[DO THE WRONG THING]]. Here's what we run instead.
Slide 3:  01 · [[POINT]] — [[ONE NUMBER]] — [[ONE LINE]]
...
Slide N:  What this adds up to: [[SYNTHESIS]]
Slide N+1: [[QUESTION]] · [[YOUR NAME]] · [[YOUR HANDLE]]
```

### Caption that accompanies a carousel

Keep it to four lines. The caption's only job is to get the tap.

```
[[THE PROBLEM, IN ONE LINE]].

[[WHAT'S IN THE CAROUSEL]].

[[WHY THIS ONE IS WORTH SAVING]].

Swipe through. [[QUESTION]]
```

## Template 4 — Project Spotlight
*600–1,100 characters. Your differentiator. Once or twice per month, no more.*

### Anatomy

```
[THE PROBLEM — stated as a human situation, not a technical one.]

[WHAT YOU BUILT — named, one sentence, no jargon.]

[THE RESULT — one number.]

[THE UNGLAMOROUS PART — what actually took the time.]

[THE FAILURE — one specific thing that went wrong.]

[WHAT IT MEANS FOR SOMEONE ELSE.]

[QUESTION.]
```

### Why the failure section is mandatory

A project spotlight without a disclosed failure reads as marketing, and the audience you
want is unusually good at detecting marketing. The failure paragraph is what converts
"nice case study" into "this person has actually done it." It is also what makes the post
safe to publish about your own products — you are reporting, not selling.

### Skeleton

```
[[THE HUMAN PROBLEM — someone couldn't do something they needed to do]].

So we built [[NAME]] — [[ONE SENTENCE, PLAIN LANGUAGE]].

[[HEADLINE METRIC: from [[BEFORE]] to [[AFTER]]]].

The part nobody warns you about: [[THE UNGLAMOROUS WORK — data cleaning, content
rewriting, process documentation]]. That was [[PROPORTION]] of the effort.

We also got [[SPECIFIC THING]] wrong. [[WHAT HAPPENED]]. [[HOW YOU FOUND OUT]].
[[WHAT YOU CHANGED]].

If you're building something similar: [[THE ONE PIECE OF ADVICE]].

[[QUESTION]]
```

### Worked example — opening

> Our drivers were handed a 200-page operations manual on day one.
>
> Nobody read it. I wouldn't have read it either.
>
> So we built Max — an AI assistant that delivers the same material as a conversation, in
> the driver's own language, on the phone already in their pocket.

## Hook bank

Twelve openings that work in an executive register. Each is a shape, not a script.

| # | Shape | Example opening |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Flat contrarian claim | *"Range anxiety is a management problem, not a battery problem."* |
| 2 | Confession of an error | *"We spent [[FIGURE]] on the wrong thing. Here's how we found out."* |
| 3 | The number alone | *"[[FIGURE]]% of our departures happen in the first [[PERIOD]]."* |
| 4 | Reframed question | *"'Will AI replace recruiters' is the least interesting question in HR."* |
| 5 | The thing nobody models | *"Nobody's EV business case has a line for August in the Gulf."* |
| 6 | Before / after | *"Onboarding: [[BEFORE]]. Today: [[AFTER]]. What changed wasn't the software."* |
| 7 | Overheard | *"A driver told me something [[TIMEFRAME]] ago that I still think about."* |
| 8 | The refusal | *"We automated five HR processes and deliberately refused to automate three."* |
| 9 | Cost disclosure | *"The build was the cheap part. Nobody tells you this."* |
| 10 | Regional specificity | *"European fleet models do not survive contact with a Gulf summer."* |
| 11 | The uncomfortable audit | *"Read your own AI policy in your workforce's second language."* |
| 12 | Inverted priority | *"We taught our AI to escalate before we taught it to answer."* |
